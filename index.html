01 Synthesis & Critical Integration of Citations — Explained!

Almost every rubric section requires this, and it is where I see the most challenges — so let us break it down.

Demonstration of synthesis and critical integration of substantial and relevant citations from topic resources and current scholarly research (published within the past 5 years) and, where appropriate, biblical sources, is clear, thorough, and insightful.

Critical Integration of Substantial & Relevant Citations

In graduate-level writing, you do not want to just slap a citation at the end of a sentence. You want to use your sources thoughtfully to build and support your ideas.

Critical integration — you explain how each source connects to your point, and you may evaluate it (point out limits, show why it matters, or explain how it supports/complicates your claim).
Substantial — you used enough credible sources and evidence to thoroughly support your points (not just one brief citation here and there).
Relevant — the citations directly relate to the specific claim you are making in that part of the paper (not a "filler" citation to satisfy the rubric).
Integration example

"While social media can increase connection, recent research suggests it may also raise anxiety when use becomes passive and comparison-based (Lopez, 2022). This supports my argument that the type of use matters more than the amount of time spent online."

Another way to phrase it: "To support my point that the type of use matters more than time spent online, I turn to current research by Lopez (2022), who found that while social media can increase connection, it may also raise anxiety when use becomes passive and comparison-based."

Any time you weave — not slap — credible citations into a statement you are making, you are integrating a citation.

Synthesis

Synthesis is when you examine where your sources agree (or disagree) and connect them to develop broader conclusions about the topic.

From Purdue OWL: Do not force a relationship between sources if there is not one — not all sources must complement one another. Highlight relationships clearly. And do not ignore outliers; every perspective matters, even those that disagree with your conclusions.
Step-by-step: building a synthesized passage ›

Working from an excerpt on self-regulation in a leader, here is how three separately-cited ideas get linked into one chain.

Sentence 1 — not common knowledge, so cite it: "This consistency can reduce uncertainty and strengthen employees' sense of security (Kunelius, 2022)."

Sentence 2 — same topic (emotions of leaders), so synthesize by linking back: "Complementing this, Bauer and Erdogan (2026) show that emotional intelligence allows leaders to navigate interpersonal dynamics with empathy and awareness, addressing common causes of turnover such as feeling undervalued or misunderstood."

Sentence 3 — same chain again: "Further, Segon and Booth (2015) state that when these competencies are grounded in ethical principles—such as integrity, compassion, and stewardship—they gain practical direction."

Wrap-up — bring all three together: "Together, consistent leadership, emotional intelligence, and ethical grounding create a stable, empathetic workplace culture that helps employees feel valued and secure—ultimately reducing turnover."

The final product reads as one continuous, well-supported argument — three sources and a conclusion, woven, not stacked.
💡
Talking about your own experience? That does not need a citation — but it can be strengthened by pairing it with a scholarly source that explains why or how. That is integration. 😊
References
Bauer, T., & Erdogan, B. (2026). Leadership and organization (5.0.1). FlatWorld.
Kunelius, V. (2022). Maintaining employee well-being during times of uncertainty.
Segon, M., & Booth, C. (2015). Virtue: The missing ethics element in emotional intelligence. Journal of Business Ethics, 128(4), 789–802.

02 Level-Up Your Writing with Synthesis

At a graduate level, you must go beyond summarizing the articles you read — you need to synthesize the literature to show how it all fits together (and how your own experience fits in). The rubrics require it.

Synthesizing simply means combining. Instead of just summarizing one author, you put together the ideas and findings of multiple sources and/or your own experience to make an overall point — looking for similarities and differences, and showing the reader where they overlap and where they diverge.

Unsynthesized

In a study of online graduate students, Franz (2008) examined 17 females and 18 males and found that more females were comfortable with APA formatting than males. While also studying graduate students, Goldstein (2012) found in a larger study that females felt more comfortable citing sources in APA than males. In experience, my female classmates seem to have a better understanding of APA than the males. (Note: these are made-up facts for illustration.)

Synthesized

Studies of graduate students reveal similar conclusions regarding relationships between scholarly study and citation efficacy. Franz (2008) found that more females were comfortable with APA formatting than males, and Goldstein (2012), in a larger study, also found that females felt more comfortable citing sources in APA than males. Both studies concur with my experience that female classmates seem to have a better understanding of APA than the males.

03 A Note on Writing & Citing: Credibility

You want a graduate degree for real reasons — advancement, leadership, credibility in your field. APA style provides a standardized format for clear, concise communication, and correct grammar keeps your work free of errors that undermine credibility. Both are things every leader needs.

Beyond avoiding plagiarism lies critical thinking. As a future graduate, you must think critically — and that starts with shedding bias and questioning your own reasoning. So when you state an opinion (says you), back it with credible evidence (says who) to check your thinking and earn credibility.

No evidence cited

"Drinking eight glasses of water a day is necessary for everyone to stay healthy." Without a credible source, this common claim may be taken as fact — even though scientific research does not universally support the "8×8" rule for all individuals (Valtin, 2002).

Cited evidence

"Regular physical activity lowers the risk of chronic diseases such as heart disease and diabetes (Warburton et al., 2006)." Citing credible research makes the statement trustworthy, lets readers verify the claim, and gives you credibility.

⚖️
If you cannot find evidence to support your statement, you probably should not be making it. That is credibility.

04 A Note on Citing Sources

You are writing at a graduate level now, so you will be held to a higher standard — and we want to avoid plagiarism at all costs. Citations support academic integrity, build credibility, strengthen your arguments, and place your work within the broader scholarly conversation.

Use an in-text citation whenever you…

Quote directly ›

Use someone's exact words → cite. "Leadership is the capacity to translate vision into reality" (Bennis, 1989). Limit direct quotes to definitions or highly complex processes.

Paraphrase or summarize ›

Put someone's ideas into your own words → still cite. Good leaders help transform a vision into actual achievements (Bennis, 1989).

Refer to data, facts, or statistics ›

Any specific data not common knowledge → cite. Employee engagement increased by 20% following the new strategy (Smith & Lee, 2021).

Refer to someone's theory or research ›

Discuss a theory, model, or result → cite. Maslow's hierarchy of needs posits that motivation is driven by fulfilling a series of needs arranged in a hierarchy (Maslow, 1943).

You do not need a citation for your own original ideas, conclusions, syntheses, or widely known facts (e.g., "Water boils at 100°C at sea level") — unless you are arguing a point and need research to back it up.

🔗
Citing a string from one source? Cite the first sentence, signal you are still in the same work ("Yang also noted that…"), and add another citation at the end of the final sentence in the string. If it is ever unclear you are still on the same source, cite again.
🤖
You must also cite AI — just as you would any other source.

05 Peer-Reviewed Is Best

Without refuting popular business or leadership "gurus" — many give excellent advice — use caution when citing or modeling your leadership after authors who do not rely on research or peer review. Lacking that quality control, some sources present unverified claims, personal opinions, or misinformation.

Peer review minimizes personal bias by having independent experts challenge assumptions and identify flaws, promoting a balanced, objective presentation. So read everything that interests you — but stay mindful of advice that may be biased:

  • Exercise caution with advice not grounded in research.
  • Be wary of guidance lacking a basis in well-established research.
  • Be discerning about claims not substantiated by evidence.
  • Approach unverified advice with skepticism — do not assume it is wrong, just be careful.
🧠
Become a critical thinker. Example: I really like Simon Sinek — inspiring! — but I know he does not research or use peer review. I still read and share him; I am just careful about what I take into practice, and I verify claims with other sources before acting on them.

06 Substantive vs. Non-Substantive Posts

Your weekly participation must be substantive — it is a large part of how you learn here. A substantive response adds value: thought-out, thought-provoking ideas, insight, analysis, or a counterpoint. A non-substantive response lacks depth, insight, and connection to the materials.

Substantive

The four principles of conscious capitalism—higher purpose, stakeholder orientation, conscious leadership, and conscious culture (Anderson, 2015)—closely connect with the Christian concept of putting others first, as seen in Philippians 2:3-4 (NIV, 2011).

Stakeholder orientation asks organizations to consider all stakeholders, not just shareholders (Anderson, 2015); a higher purpose gives meaning beyond profit (Mackey & Sisodia, 2014); conscious leadership promotes servant leadership (Greenleaf, 1977; Mackey & Sisodia, 2014); and a conscious culture supports respect and collaboration. Both frameworks emphasize empathy, service, and the impact of one's actions on others.

Non-substantive

"I think the four principles of conscious capitalism and the Christian concept of putting others first are pretty similar. They both talk about helping people and doing the right thing. There are a lot of connections."

See the full worked example ›

Question: What are your thoughts on the role of EI in leadership? Have you encountered examples where EI made a significant difference?

Thoughts on EI in Leadership

Emotional intelligence (EI) plays a critical role in effective leadership. According to Goleman (1995), EI encompasses self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, and social skills — all essential for managing teams and fostering a positive environment. Leaders with high EI are better equipped to handle conflict, inspire teams, and adapt (Bradberry & Greaves, 2009).

My Experience

In my own experience, I observed a manager who demonstrated high EI by actively listening to employees' concerns and addressing them empathetically. This improved team morale and productivity. Research supports this, as leaders with strong EI positively influence organizational outcomes (Cherniss, 2010).

In conclusion, emotional intelligence is a vital component of leadership. By cultivating EI, leaders create a more collaborative and effective workplace.

References
Bradberry, T., & Greaves, J. (2009). Emotional intelligence 2.0. TalentSmart.
Cherniss, C. (2010). Emotional intelligence: Toward clarification of a concept. Industrial and Organizational Psychology, 3(2), 110–126.
Goleman, D. (1995). Emotional intelligence: Why it can matter more than IQ. Bantam Books.

08 Contractions!

Do not use contractions in academic writing. Because so many former students were not sure what counted, here is the reminder: contractions are two words shortened and combined with an apostrophe — for example, do not becomes don't, cannot becomes can't, and it is becomes it's. Those shortened forms are reserved for informal communication and should be avoided here.

🎓
Contractions are generally fine in undergraduate work, but graduate-level writing requires a more professional tone that reflects the standards of someone pursuing an advanced degree.

09 Artificial Intelligence (AI)

AI Anxiety

Some of you may feel anxiety or confusion about AI — that is normal; we are all learning this tool in academia. The first time I see inappropriate use, I will call it out, but I will not discount your grade (unless it is an egregious abuse). GCU has short FAQ videos covering learning verification, disclosure, academic integrity, authorship, appropriate vs. inappropriate use, and why GCU does not use AI detectors.

GCU's AI Policy

GCU allows certain uses of AI. Using AI to assist with classwork and support your learning is encouraged — brainstorming, finding a better way to say something, linking thoughts, checking grammar — provided it enhances your work without replacing your understanding.

Not allowed

Using AI to do your work for you. This inhibits learning and is academic dishonesty. You must maintain ownership of your coursework — the thoughts and analyses must be your own.

Allowed (with disclosure)

AI-assisted learning: "help me understand this concept," "help me see this another way," "help me say this better." Anytime you use AI, you must clearly disclose how — summarize its role and attribute ideas or phrasing that came from it. Transparency is part of academic integrity.

Proper (and Not-So-Proper) Use of AI

⛔
Do not use AI to find sources after you write. Hunting for articles that agree with what you already said flips the academic process backward — sources should inform and shape your thinking, not validate it after the fact.

Here is why it matters:

  • It encourages confirmation bias. Looking only for agreement means you miss stronger evidence, key limitations, or credible disagreement.
  • It reduces real learning. Growth comes from reading, comparing perspectives, and refining your stance based on what research actually shows.
  • It leads to shallow citations. Sources chosen after the fact get forced into places they do not fit — that is when I comment, "says who?"
  • It undermines integrity. Generating or cherry-picking references can blur the line between your scholarship and AI output, especially if sources are summarized inaccurately or unread.

A better approach: start with a question, use sources to map what credible authors say, then write your point and cite the most relevant source(s). If you do draft first, the next step is not "find a source that agrees" — it is "check whether research supports, complicates, or contradicts this, and revise accordingly."

Citing AI vs. Attributing AI

Citing AI — formally referencing AI-generated content, like a book or article, when you directly quote, paraphrase, or incorporate it. Treat the tool as author, include the model/platform, date of access, and URL.

Reference: OpenAI. (2025, March 20). ChatGPT [Large language model]. https://chat.openai.com/
In-text: (OpenAI, 2026).
Attributing AI — a broader acknowledgment of the tool's role without a formal reference. Put a note after the last paragraph of the section where it was used.

Example: "I used ChatGPT to generate initial summaries of key concepts, which informed my writing; however, I did not quote or paraphrase any of the material."
